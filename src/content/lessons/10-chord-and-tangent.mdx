## Where we are

In Lesson 3 one rational point on a circle gave us every rational point: draw a line with rational slope through it, and the *other* place the line meets the circle is again rational. The reason was simple algebra. A line meets a conic where a quadratic equation holds, and if a quadratic with rational coefficients has one rational root, its other root is rational too.

Now try the same on a cubic curve such as

$$
y^2 = x^3 - 7x + 10 .
$$

A line meets a cubic where a **cubic** equation holds, so there are three meeting points (Lesson 8). Knowing one rational root of a cubic tells you almost nothing about the other two: they can be a pair like $1 \pm \sqrt{2}$, irrational, but adding up to something rational. One point is not enough.

Two points are.

## By the end of this lesson you can

- explain why the line through two rational points of a cubic meets it in a third rational point;
- carry out the chord construction and the tangent construction by hand, with exact fractions;
- say what the point $O$ is, and why "reflect in the $x$-axis" is the same as "join to $O$";
- check three of the four group axioms for this addition, and say which one is still owed.

## The key fact: two rational points give a third

Take a cubic curve whose equation has rational coefficients, and two rational points $P$ and $Q$ on it. The line through them has a rational slope and a rational intercept, so it is $y = \lambda x + \nu$ with $\lambda, \nu$ rational. Substitute that into the curve's equation. You get a cubic equation in $x$ alone, with rational coefficients:

$$
x^3 + c_2 x^2 + c_1 x + c_0 = 0 .
$$

Its three roots are the $x$-coordinates of the three meeting points. Call them $x_1, x_2, x_3$. Then the cubic factors as $(x - x_1)(x - x_2)(x - x_3)$, and multiplying out and comparing the $x^2$ terms gives

$$
x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = -c_2 .
$$

This is the <Term id="sum-of-roots">sum of the roots</Term> rule. We know $x_1$ and $x_2$: they belong to $P$ and $Q$, and they are rational. So $x_3 = -c_2 - x_1 - x_2$ is rational, and then $y_3 = \lambda x_3 + \nu$ is rational as well.

<Callout title="Notation" kind="note">
The third meeting point of the line through $P$ and $Q$ is written $P * Q$. Read it as "$P$ star $Q$".
</Callout>

### Worked numbers

On $y^2 = x^3 - 7x + 10$ take $P = (1, 2)$ and $Q = (3, 4)$. Both are on the curve: $1 - 7 + 10 = 4 = 2^2$ and $27 - 21 + 10 = 16 = 4^2$.

The line through them has slope $(4 - 2)/(3 - 1) = 1$, so it is $y = x + 1$. Substituting,

$$
(x + 1)^2 = x^3 - 7x + 10
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x^3 - x^2 - 9x + 9 = 0 .
$$

The roots add up to $1$ (minus the coefficient of $x^2$). Two of them are $1$ and $3$, so the third is $1 - 1 - 3 = -3$, and on the line $y = -3 + 1 = -2$. So

$$
P * Q = (-3, -2).
$$

Check it against the curve: $(-3)^3 - 7(-3) + 10 = -27 + 21 + 10 = 4 = (-2)^2$. We never solved a cubic. We only used the sum of its roots.

## Star is not yet addition

It is tempting to declare $P * Q$ to be "the sum" and stop. Lesson 9 says what a sum must satisfy, and $*$ fails at the first hurdle: there is no identity element.

An identity would be a point $E$ with $P * E = P$ for every $P$. Unpack that. The line through $P$ and $E$ would have to meet the curve at $P$, at $E$, and at $P$ *again*. A line that meets the curve twice at $P$ is the <Term id="tangent-line">tangent line</Term> at $P$. So the tangent line at every single point of the curve would have to pass through the one point $E$. Tangent lines to a cubic do not all pass through a common point, so no such $E$ exists.

## The repair: choose a point and call it O

Pick one point on the curve, once and for all, and name it $O$. Define

$$
P + Q \;=\; O * (P * Q).
$$

In words: join $P$ and $Q$, take the third point; then join *that* to $O$ and take the third point again.

**$O$ is the identity.** Compute $P + O = O * (P * O)$. The line through $P$ and $O$ meets the curve at a third point; call it $T$. Now join $O$ to $T$. That is the *same line*, and its three meeting points are $O$, $T$ and $P$. The third one is $P$. So $P + O = P$.

**The order does not matter** (the operation is <Term id="commutative">commutative</Term>). The line through $P$ and $Q$ is the line through $Q$ and $P$, so $P * Q = Q * P$ and therefore $P + Q = Q + P$.

**Every point has a negative.** We will see this most easily in the special position described next.

## Where O lives in our pictures

The pages you will read in the book let $O$ be any point of a general cubic, exactly as above. The widget below can do that too. But there is one choice of curve and of $O$ that makes everything simpler, and from Lesson 12 on we always make it.

Lesson 12 shows that any cubic with a rational point can be brought to the form $y^2 = x^3 + ax^2 + bx + c$, and that a shift in $x$ then removes the $x^2$ term to give $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$. Every curve in this lesson is already in that last form (see <Term id="weierstrass-form">Weierstrass form</Term>). For these curves we take $O$ to be the point at infinity in the vertical direction, the place where all vertical lines meet. Lesson 12 also shows that this $O$ really is a point of the curve.

With $O$ straight up at infinity, "the line through $O$ and $T$" is simply **the vertical line through $T$**. Where else does a vertical line meet $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$? If $(x, y)$ is on the curve then so is $(x, -y)$, because only $y^2$ appears. So the third point is the mirror image of $T$ in the $x$-axis.

That turns the definition into a recipe you can do with a ruler:

1. Draw the line through $P$ and $Q$.
2. Find the third point $P * Q$ where it meets the curve.
3. Reflect it in the $x$-axis. That is $P + Q$.

For the worked example, $P * Q = (-3, -2)$, so

$$
(1, 2) + (3, 4) = (-3, 2).
$$

**Negatives.** The mirror image of $P = (x, y)$ is the point $(x, -y)$. The line joining them is vertical, so it meets the curve at $P$, at its mirror image, and at $O$. Hence $P * (x, -y) = O$, and joining $O$ to $O$ and reflecting gives $O$ again. So $P + (x, -y) = O$: the mirror image is $-P$.

## When P and Q coincide: the tangent

What is $P + P$? There is no "line through $P$ and $P$" unless we say which one. The right choice is the **tangent line** at $P$. A tangent touches the curve twice at $P$ (Lesson 8), so it is the limit of the chords through $P$ and a second point sliding toward it. Its third meeting point is $P * P$, and reflecting gives $P + P$, written $2P$.

For the slope, use <Term id="implicit-differentiation">implicit differentiation</Term> on $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$: $2y\,y' = 3x^2 + a$, so

$$
\lambda = \frac{3x^2 + a}{2y} .
$$

### Worked numbers

On $y^2 = x^3 - 2$ take $P = (3, 5)$; indeed $27 - 2 = 25$. The tangent slope is $\lambda = 27/10$. Substituting the tangent line into the curve gives a cubic whose $x^2$ coefficient is $-\lambda^2$, and whose roots are $3$, $3$ and the unknown $x_3$. So

$$
x_3 = \lambda^2 - 3 - 3 = \frac{729}{100} - 6 = \frac{129}{100},
\qquad
y_3 = 5 + \frac{27}{10}\left(\frac{129}{100} - 3\right) = \frac{383}{1000}.
$$

Reflect:

$$
2P = \left(\frac{129}{100},\; -\frac{383}{1000}\right).
$$

A point with one-digit coordinates has become one with three- and four-digit fractions in a single step. Keep that in mind. The speed at which the digits grow is the central tool of Part III.

**What is drawn.** The dark curve is the set of real solutions of the equation on the selected button. The orange point is $P$ and the green point is $Q$. The amber line is the line through them. The pink point is the third meeting point $P * Q$. The dashed blue segment is the reflection, and the blue point is the answer, $P + Q$. The picture rescales itself so that the whole construction stays in view.

**What to try.**

1. Press **Replay** and watch the three steps for $(1,2) + (3,4)$: the line is drawn, a marker runs along it to the third point, and that point is carried across the $x$-axis. The strip should read $(-3, 2)$, the value computed above. **Back** and **Next step** let you take it one step at a time.
2. Tap a hollow dot to send $P$ there, or drag $P$ and $Q$ and drop them on dots. Each time, both the third point and the sum land exactly on the curve.
3. Drag $Q$ until it sits directly below $P$ (same $x$, opposite $y$). Watch the line become vertical.
4. Press **Set Q = P**. The line becomes dashed: it is now the tangent.
5. Choose $y^2 = x^3 - 2$. It opens already doubling $(3, 5)$. Then press **Move P to 2P** and keep adding, and watch the fractions.
6. Choose **Custom** and set $a = -3$, $b = 2$.
7. Go back to the first curve and press **Choose your own O**. A black square $O$ appears on the curve; this is the situation in the book's pictures. Step through the construction: step 3 is now "join $P * Q$ to $O$ and take the third point". Then drag $Q$ onto $O$.

**What to notice.**

- When every point is on a hollow dot, every number is an exact fraction. This is the key fact of this lesson, seen directly: rational in, rational out.
- When a point is somewhere else on the curve, its coordinates are usually irrational, and the readout switches to decimals marked with ≈.
- In try 3, the third point runs off to infinity and the strip reads $O$. A point plus its mirror image is $O$.
- In try 5, numerators and denominators roughly quadruple in length with each doubling.
- In try 6 the curve has a crossing point and the construction is switched off. There $4a^3 + 27b^2 = 0$. Such curves are called singular; a <Term id="non-singular">non-singular</Term> curve is one where this does not happen, and only those are allowed in this lesson.
- In try 7, when $Q$ sits on $O$ the answer sits on $P$. Whatever point you choose as $O$ is the identity. The sums themselves change when you move $O$, but the rules they obey do not. With $O$ at infinity, step 3 collapses to a mirror image, which is why that choice is so convenient.

**The exact mathematics behind it.** For $P = (x_1, y_1)$ and $Q = (x_2, y_2)$ on $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$, the widget computes

$$
\lambda = \begin{cases} \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} & \text{if } x_1 \ne x_2, \\[2ex] \dfrac{3x_1^2 + a}{2y_1} & \text{if } P = Q \text{ and } y_1 \ne 0, \end{cases}
\qquad
x_3 = \lambda^2 - x_1 - x_2,
\qquad
y_3 = \lambda(x_1 - x_3) - y_1 ,
$$

and reports $P * Q = (x_3, -y_3)$ and, when $O$ is at infinity, $P + Q = (x_3, y_3)$. With your own $O$ it applies the same line-and-third-point step a second time, to $O$ and $P * Q$. The formula for $x_3$ is the sum-of-roots fact again: substituting $y = \lambda x + \nu$ gives a cubic whose $x^2$ coefficient is $-\lambda^2$. Lesson 13 derives these formulas in full. For exact points the arithmetic is done on whole-number numerators and denominators of any size, so nothing is rounded.

**Edge cases.**

- $x_1 = x_2$ but $y_1 = -y_2$: the line is vertical and $P + Q = O$.
- $P = Q$ with $y_1 = 0$: the tangent itself is vertical, so $2P = O$. Try it on $y^2 = x^3 - x$ with $P = Q = (0, 0)$. Such points get their own lesson (16).
- With your own $O$, either $P * Q$ or $P + Q$ can be the point at infinity. It is then an ordinary point of the curve, no longer the identity, and the widget says so.
- If a point is extremely far away the picture stops growing and says so. The numbers stay exact.
- Dragging moves a point to the nearest place on the curve, and snaps to a hollow dot when it is within a small distance. The picture holds still while your finger is down and refits when you let go.

## The statement, and what is still owed

<Callout title="Theorem (completed in Lesson 11)" kind="ok">
Let $C$ be a <Term id="non-singular">non-singular</Term> cubic curve and $O$ a point on it. With $P + Q = O * (P * Q)$, the points of $C$ form a commutative group with identity $O$. If the equation of $C$ has rational coefficients and $O$ is a rational point, then the rational points of $C$ form a <Term id="subgroup">subgroup</Term>: the sum of two rational points is rational, and so is the negative of one.
</Callout>

Here is the scorecard against the axioms of Lesson 9.

| Axiom | Status |
|---|---|
| The sum of two points is a point of the curve | Done: it is a third meeting point |
| $P + Q = Q + P$ | Done |
| $P + O = P$ | Done |
| Every $P$ has a $-P$ with $P + (-P) = O$ | Done (mirror image) |
| $(P + Q) + R = P + (Q + R)$ | **Not yet.** This is Lesson 11 |

The last line is not a formality. Nothing in the ruler construction makes it obvious that adding three points in two different orders ends in the same place, and proving it takes a real geometric idea about nine points on a cubic.

<Callout title="Two honest gaps" kind="warn">
Two things were used and not proved here. First, that a line meets a cubic in exactly three points; this needs points at infinity and tangency counted properly (Lessons 6–8). Second, that the point at infinity really lies on $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$ and behaves like any other point (Lesson 12).
</Callout>

## Where this leads

- **Lesson 11** pays the debt: associativity.
- **Lesson 12** shows that any cubic with a rational point can be brought to the form $y^2 = x^3 + ax^2 + bx + c$, and that a shift in $x$ then removes the $x^2$ term to give $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$. It also puts $O$ at infinity.
